## The starting line

At the start of 2011 I joined an ecommerce operation as a Systems Administrator, filling a vacancy left open by a
long-time employee who had recently left the organization. I was tasked with making sense of the previous Sys Admin’s
kingdom - dozens of desktop workstations, a server room full of Linux and Windows machines, and other critical business
systems that supported daily operations - all held together by layers of duct tape. I was the IT department in its
entirety, and balanced time between unjamming printers (I’ve developed quite a passionate _hatred_ for printers as a
result) and maintaining the company’s aging technology infrastructure. Things were left in quite the chaotic state -
outdated servers running operating systems years past end-of-life, workstations still on Windows 2000, and legacy
productivity software nobody had touched in years. There was no engineering team, no technology strategy, and no one
thinking about technology as a business function.

My job was simple - bring order to chaos.

## Earning trust by delivering

Updating systems and detangling the web was straightforward - and what was expected of me. However, it didn’t take long
to start seeing opportunities that would improve and enhance the company’s technology infrastructure.

The company’s server room was bursting at the seams with physical servers - each one unique with its own arsenal of
replacement parts and specific know-how. I quickly realized transforming everything into a virtualization cluster would
significantly reduce complexity, cooling, and electricity demand. I got to work on building a XenServer virtualization
cluster, sourcing two identical “big iron” servers and virtualizing each physical machine one by one - often after hours
to eliminate business disruption. Before long, the entire server room was reduced to the two-server cluster, with the
exception of the company’s IBM AS/400 ERP system and a dedicated storage server which ensured redundancy and high
availability. The server room now almost looked like an empty room, despite maintaining full business capability. We
even had excess capacity, which gave us room to grow without buying new hardware.

Similarly, the company used an antiquated system backup scheme. No servers were backed up, and only some workstations
had a local backup system running. The existing system would overwrite the previous night’s backup, dutifully backing up
corrupted files and erroneous user changes. Files the user had deleted were purged from the backup as well, further
reducing the system’s effectiveness. I realized a better system needed to be set up - _quickly_ - before disaster
struck. I set up an open source backup solution that covered every machine in the building, Linux and Windows, servers
and workstations. It did versioned backups with deduplication, required no client-side software, and gave users the
ability to restore their own files. No more overwritten backups, no more lost work.

## Building what didn’t exist

While the company was a traditional ecommerce company, with all revenue generated by its only sales channel - its
website - I dreamed of something bigger. I recognized an opportunity to expand onto online marketplaces, with the
eventual goal of transforming the business into a true omni-channel operation - selling our products anywhere customers
were looking for them. Marketplaces like Amazon had existed for some time, but these were still early days and most
customers in our industry weren't shopping there yet. Company leadership was hesitant - we were one of the major players
in our industry, and there was concern marketplaces would be a distraction. No other competitors had a presence outside
their website, and the thinking was to stick with what the company already knew and was very good at. I was convinced
the future was multi-channel commerce.

I was still pretty junior, had no real authority, and business expansion was well outside my job description. I knew I
had to form a rock-solid plan before leadership would greenlight anything. I got in contact with an Amazon onboarding
representative. At the time, Amazon was actively recruiting established ecommerce businesses onto their marketplace, and
they were eager to help us get set up. The representative taught me everything I’d need to know about operating on the
platform, and together we crafted a high-quality product feed using a small subset of our product catalog, which would
serve as a proof of concept. I worked out a plan for every detail: order ingestion, internal fulfillment workflows,
customer service, and inventory synchronization. Much of it would fall on me to handle personally at first, in addition
to my regular duties. I was the one answering customer emails, processing returns, manually typing orders into our ERP
system, managing the product catalog, and figuring out how the marketplace actually worked. The best way to build the
playbook was to do the work myself. I took my plan to company leadership and got the greenlight.

Success became apparent very quickly - within weeks I was spending significant time managing our Amazon presence instead
of my regular duties. There was no putting the genie back in the bottle.

I needed to build actual infrastructure to support the new sales channel in anticipation of full-catalog expansion onto
the platform. I knew our normal external contractors would be cost-prohibitive even for this stage of success - so I
decided to teach myself to program on my own time, staying up late and burning every weekend available. I discovered I
_absolutely loved_ programming - something that hadn't even been on my radar. That discovery would eventually lead me to
pursue a Bachelor's in Computer Science, but that's a story for another time. I worked hard to cobble together the
initial integration system that would support the company’s first marketplace. It wasn’t a great system, and looking
back on it now I don’t even know what possessed me (a _Systems Administrator_ of all things) to believe I could pull
that off - but, _I did_.

I had pushed leadership past their comfort zone, and ultimately built a completely new line of business - one that
eventually grew into a seven-figure annual revenue sales channel. I had changed the company’s trajectory, forever.

## Leading beyond technology

I moved from Systems Administrator to Engineering Lead to Director and eventually CTO after a long tenure of
consistently tackling hard problems and delivering results nobody knew they needed or asked for. I didn’t just stick to
technology and engineering - wherever I saw a problem or opportunity, I worked on a solution. This often meant embedding
with teams across the organization, learning their daily routines, processes, and pain points - not just observing, but
doing the work alongside them. You learn things on a warehouse floor that no spreadsheet or meeting will ever reveal.

I owe this and much of my leadership philosophy to my long-time mentor and former supervisor, Tony Roberts, who took me
under his wing and showed me the business side of the company. He expanded my perspective well beyond technology and
showed me how the business actually worked. I wouldn't be the leader I am today without his influence.

As the company transformed and restructured over the years - adapting to changing market forces and environments - it
was natural for me to increasingly lead cross-functional teams organization-wide. I would eventually come to lead
warehouse operations, customer service, creative services, marketing, as well as numerous external contractors and
vendor teams. I also handled vendor and service provider relationships, contract negotiations, daily operations, and
strategic business initiatives.

Some of the most unexpected things I took on had nothing to do with technology at all. I secured three trademarks via
direct filing and negotiations with USPTO Examining Attorneys - two of which experienced significant challenges - all
without hiring a lawyer to handle the details on the company’s behalf. _Lawyers… who needs them?_ Well, except for when
the company faced external legal challenges. I guided the company through ADA/WCAG compliance, California Prop 65, and
consumer protection claims - conducting legal research, coordinating with outside counsel, and advising on resolution
strategy.

I’ll always be a technology and engineering enthusiast at heart, but some of my most significant growth came from
leading outside that comfort zone.

## The big transformation

The company’s IBM AS/400-based ERP system was decades old, riddled with technical debt, and had no upgrade path. Nearly
every business process was tightly coupled to it. Migrating a system like this is high stakes - get it wrong and the
business grinds to a halt. Before evaluating replacements, I embedded with every team in the organization, rolling up my
sleeves alongside warehouse operations, customer service, purchasing, and accounting. It was one thing to study
processes from a distance, another entirely to do the work shoulder-to-shoulder with the people who lived in the system
every day.

After an exhaustive evaluation process, I selected the replacement system and began training teams in parallel with
legacy operations. Change is hard, especially for people who have used the same system since its inception. I built
targeted demos for each team that highlighted their specific workflow improvements, making sure they knew their feedback
had directly shaped the selection. By go-live, every team was ready to hit the ground running.

The go-live happened over a company holiday. No downtime, no lost data. Teams came back the next business day and
started using the new system without hesitation. Well, except for one major incident. We couldn’t ship anything! Our new
ERP and shipping systems hadn’t been fully testable together until both were live, and the integration wasn’t working.
For a few tense hours, orders piled up with no way out the door. The shipping team adapted on the fly, jumping onto the
floor to pick orders while we worked through the issues, then it was all hands on deck to clear the backlog and hit our
carrier cutoff. We made it!

I remember expecting more fires to extinguish, but instead feeling like a proud coach watching his team win a
championship from the sidelines. Every single person in that building had completely relearned how they did their job.
Every process, every workflow, every piece of institutional knowledge, changed. What we pulled off together was
something special.

## The tension between building and leading

Advancing into leadership often means trading hands-on craftsmanship for the opportunity to shape outcomes across the
organization. For me, that meant less time doing some of the things I loved most - such as writing code and building
applications. The temptation to build is strong because it’s more fun, potentially cheaper long-term, and gives you full
control. However, I increasingly realized the company needed my focus elsewhere. This often meant buying services and
applications or delegating projects to external contractors instead of building things myself.

Handing the reins over to external contractors was particularly difficult for me. Buying an off-the-shelf solution was
easy to accept. Shifting from defining the “what and how” to just the “what” was much harder. Early on, if a project
wasn’t done the way I would have approached it - it was _“wrong”_. I had to learn to accept others’ work and judge based
on results and outcome instead of particulars of the implementation. My way isn’t always the right way. That took a
while to internalize.

My job became defining what needed to be done and providing guidance, then getting out of the way. The key, I learned,
is to set clear expectations, foster open and frequent communication, measure results, and maintain accountability. I
still catch myself wanting to jump in and rewrite things. But I’ve learned that a good result I didn’t build myself is
worth more than a perfect result only I could maintain.

## What I’d do differently

_”No plan survives first contact…”_, as the saying goes, and the roll-out of a new product filtering feature on the
company’s website certainly exemplified this notion. Months were spent transforming and structuring product data into
something compatible with a product filtering service, including building custom in-house software to facilitate the
endeavor. The feature was tested thoroughly, and all stakeholders were on board. We launched… and revenue fell off a
cliff. Even worse, we had no idea why. At the time, we lacked observability tooling that would have provided critical
insight into the reasons behind the sudden decline, which meant we were left guessing. Our best hunch was the sudden
change was difficult for some customers, but would be a temporary blip as people became familiar with how to navigate
and use the website. Weeks without improvement, and no data to guide us - we made the painful decision to pull the plug.
Months of work unraveled in a single afternoon. That day, I learned a painful lesson - before making significant
changes, ensure you’ve measured a baseline for later comparison, ensure you have adequate observability into user
behavior, and follow up with measurements to better inform decision making. Our failure to follow these core principles
led to an unfortunate outcome. I won’t make that mistake again.

Compliance, whether regulatory, contractual, assurance, or internal, is something that should be thought of from the
very start - another lesson I learned the hard way. In many organizations if compliance is considered at all, it's often
as an afterthought. Things are built first, and then later compliance is bolted on. This creates cognitive dissonance,
where compliance is viewed as a necessary burden - a chore that gets deferred until it can’t be ignored. The company
fell into this trap - and paid (_literally_) for it. My attitude on compliance has changed as a result, and today I like
to think of compliance as part of the way we build things. Instead of viewing compliance as something to worry about
later, I incorporate it into the process from the very start. The question goes from _”how do we make X compliant?”_ to
_”how do we build X in a compliant way?”_ This subtle shift removes the dissonance and burden - compliance becomes part
of the _“right way”_ to do things. No longer is compliance an afterthought - it’s the forethought.

People appreciate honesty, directness, and transparency. But I’ve learned to state my case and then let go. I struggled
with the _”letting go”_ part for a while - perhaps still do to an extent. It can be difficult to let go when convinced
the right path isn’t being followed - but I’ve learned I’m not always as right as I think I am, and it’s better to share
my perspective and then listen. You just might learn a thing or two.

## The pattern

Looking back, this pattern didn’t start here. It’s how I’ve always operated. Immerse myself and learn from the people
doing the work. Roll up my sleeves and dive in. Develop as complete an understanding as possible so I can see the bigger
picture. Listen more than I speak. Be open to ideas that challenge my assumptions. Do the work nobody else knows needs
doing. And always seek out the next thing instead of waiting for it to come to me.

I have a lot left to learn. I look forward to whatever challenges the future may bring, and the ways I’ll continue to
develop myself as a leader - and as a person. Not only do I strive to hone my craft in technology, engineering, and
business - I strive to become a more well-rounded, wiser, better-balanced individual who others are proud to work
alongside.

Here’s to what comes next.
